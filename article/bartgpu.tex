\begin{abstract}

	Bayesian Additive Regression Trees (BART) is a nonparametric Bayesian
	regression technique based on an ensemble of decision trees. It is part of
	the toolbox of many statisticians. The overall statistical quality of the
	regression is typically higher than other generic alternatives, and it
	requires less manual tuning, making it a good default choice. However, it is
	a niche method compared to natural competitors such as \texttt{XGBoost}, due
	to the longer running time, making sample sizes above
	\num{10000}--\num{100000} a nuisance. We present a GPU-enabled
	implementation of BART, faster by up to 100x in a given GPU vs.\ CPU
	comparison, making BART fast enough to be a viable alternative to
	non-Bayesian algorithms on large datasets, and showing that BART
	parallelizes better than its non-Bayesian counterparts. This implementation
	is available in the Python package \texttt{bartz}.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Bayesian Additive Regression Trees (BART) is a nonparametric Bayesian regression
method, introduced by \citet{chipman2006,chipman2010}. It defines a prior
distribution over the space of functions by representing them as a sum of binary
decision trees, and then specifying a stochastic tree generation process. The
posterior is then obtained with Metropolis-Gibbs sampling over the trees. BART
can be considered as a Bayesian version of tree boosting. See \citet{hill2020}
for a review, and \citet[ch.~5]{daniels2023} for a textbook treatment.

BART is empirically effective and gained some popularity \citep[consider,
	e.g.,][]{tan2019}. The Atlantic Causal Inference Conference (ACIC) Data
Challenge confirmed BART as one of the best regression methods for causal
inference \citep{dorie2019,hahn2019,acic2019,thal2023}. A good part of the BART
literature focuses on adding features to the core algorithm, e.g., missing
predictors \citep{kapelner_prediction_2015}, variable selection
\citep{linero2018}, smooth prediction \citep{linero2018b}, discrete and
categorical outcomes \citep{murray_log-linear_2021}, dynamic number of trees
\citep[ch.~3]{collins_improved_2023}, categorical predictors
\citep{deshpande_flexbart_2025}, and arbitrary conditional response distribution
(i.e., arbitrary ``likelihood'' or ``loss'') \citep{linero_generalized_2025}.
Other works design models built on top of BART to handle specific needs, e.g.,
causal inference \citep{hahn2020}, missing data problems \citep{goh_joint_2024},
and time series \citep{huber_inference_2022,lima_minnesota_2025}. Some effort
was directed at computational optimization: data parallelism
\citep{pratola2014}, efficient implementations
\citep{dorie2024,deshpande_flexbart_2025}, and Accelerated BART (XBART)
\citep{he2019,he_stochastic_2023}.

The natural non-Bayesian competitors to compare BART against are popular
gradient-boosted trees implementations like XGBoost \citep{chen2016}, LightGBM
\citep{ke_lightgbm_2017} and CatBoost \citep{dorogush_catboost_2018}.
\citet[table~2, p.~1105]{linero2018b} and \citet{he_stochastic_2023} include
systematic comparisons against XGBoost showing that BART achieves higher
predictive performance, while working well without tuning. Furthermore, as a
Bayesian method, BART includes out-of-the-box uncertainty quantification, and,
as illustrated by the many variants in the literature, it can be extended to
larger models in a principled way, making BART a solid building block. Despite
these positive factors, BART is a niche method compared to its non-Bayesian
counterparts. According to the \citet[p.~35]{kaggle2021} survey, the most
popular regression tool amongst the ones mentioned is XGBoost, used by half of
the surveyed data scientists. From download counts, we estimate that BART is
$O(1000)$ times less used that XGBoost.

We hypothesize the following reasons for this gap, in decreasing order of
importance: 1) Speed: a single BART run produces an ensemble of $\mathord{\sim}
	1000$ forests, representing the posterior distribution. Accordingly, BART is
$O(1000)$ times slower. The largest BART analysis we know of had
$n\approx\num{500000}$ observations/examples and $p=90$ predictors/features
\citep{pratola2020}, while \texttt{XGBoost} boasts ``billions of examples'' on
its home page. 2) Classification performance: the literature lacks a systematic
investigation of the performance of BART with binary rather than continuos
outcomes; it could be that in this setting BART is not any better than popular
methods. 3) Software ecosystem: even though \citet{he_stochastic_2023} showed
how XBART is fast enough to be an alternative to non-Bayesian methods, the
software implementation was not maintained to the quality standards of popular
data science packages, with only recent efforts at providing a well-maintained
package \citep{herren_stochtree_2026}.

This paper re-addresses the speed gap by re-implementing BART on GPU. While
XBART is 20--30 times faster than BART at the cost of renouncing a well-defined
statistical model, our plain BART implementation is 100 times faster on GPU vs.\
CPU, while achieving state-of-the-art speed on CPU (at $n \gtrsim
	\num{1000000}$, on a Nvidia RTX Pro 5000 Blackwell vs.\ an Apple M1 Pro). This
makes BART fast enough to be a (still generally slower) alternative to
non-Bayesian methods on large datasets without methodological caveats, but with
the inconvenience of requiring a GPU.\footnote{In a previous version of this
	manuscript, we claimed running time ``comparable'' to XGBoost, and a 200x
	speedup vs.\ cpu. We moderated these claims as both XGBoost and our cpu
	implementation became faster in the intervening time before this revision, while
	the speed on gpu at high $n$ has not changed.} While XGBoost \& co.{} have their
own GPU implementations as well, we find that BART achieves higher speed ups
with parallelization. We provide a Python package, \texttt{bartz}
\redactedcite{\citep{gattocrucco_2026_19661598}}. In \autoref{sec:bart} we summarize the
background knowledge on BART, then in \autoref{sec:impl} we describe how to
adapt BART to run on GPU, and in \autoref{sec:perf} we measure the performance
of our implementation. Finally, in \autoref{sec:conclusions} we assess the
merits of our work and consider further research directions.

\section{BART recap}
\label{sec:bart}

BART is a Bayesian nonparametric regression method. Semi-formally, this means
that, given a set of $n$ pairs of observations $(\mathbf x_i,y_i) \in \mathbb
	R^p \times \mathbb R$, it infers without guidance a function $f$ such that $y_i
	= f(\mathbf x_i) + \varepsilon_i$, keeping a balance between $f$ and the error
terms $\varepsilon_i$ such that given a new $\mathbf x^*$ from a pair $(\mathbf
	x^*, y^*)$ with $y^*$ yet unknown, $f(\mathbf x^*)$ is a good advance prediction
of $y^*$. The result is provided in the form of a set of possible functions
$\{f_k\}$, chosen in such a way that the spread of $f_k(\mathbf x^*)$ over the
possibilities quantifies the uncertainty in predicting $y^*$. More precisely,
this ensemble is obtained by defining a prior distribution over the space of
functions, and then sampling the corresponding posterior distribution. See
\citet[ch.~4]{muller2015} for a general reference on this topic.

BART represents the regression function as the sum of a fixed number
$n_\text{tree}$ of decision trees. The prior on functions is induced by defining
a single-tree prior recursively, which is repeated i.i.d.\ for all trees. The
posterior is sampled by a BART-specific MCMC algorithm that updates one tree at
a time. The most popular implementations of BART are the R software libraries
\texttt{bartMachine} \citep{kapelner2016,kapelner2023}, \texttt{dbarts}
\citep{dorie2024}, and \texttt{BART} \citep{sparapani2021,mcculloch2024}, though
many others exist. In the following, we summarize the description from the
original BART paper \citep{chipman2010}.

\paragraph{Decision trees}

A decision tree is a way to represent a stepwise function $\mathbb R^p \to \mathbb R$
using a binary tree. Each non-leaf node contains a \emph{decision rule}, consisting in a
pair $(i, s)$ combining a splitting axis $i$ with a splitting point $s$ along such axis,
partitioning the input space in two half-spaces along the plane $\{\mathbf x \in \mathbb
	R^p: x_i = s\}$, orthogonal to axis $i$. Each half is assigned to a children of the node.
Children nodes in turn further divide their space amongst their own children. Leaf nodes
contain the constant function value to be associated to their whole assigned rectangular
subregion. A minimal tree consisting of a single root leaf node represents a flat
function. See \autoref{fig:tree} for an illustration.

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=5in]{fig1hill2020.pdf}
	\caption{\label{fig:tree} Depiction and terminology of a decision tree. Adapted from
		\citet{hill2020}.}
\end{figure}

A decision tree can be used to represent a parametrized family of functions for
regression. The parameters are the structure of the tree $T$, including splitting
decisions, and the leaf values $M$. We indicate the function represented by the tree with
$g(\mathbf x;T,M)$. To use this model in Bayesian inference, it is necessary to specify a
prior distribution over $(T,M)$. We factorize the prior as $p(T,M) = p(M\mid T) p(T)$ and
specify the two parts separately.

\paragraph{Prior over tree structure}

The distribution $p(T)$ is defined by the following recursive generative model
\citep[see][]{chipman1998}. Fix an axes-aligned grid in the input space, not necessarily
evenly spaced, by specifying a set of splitting points $S_i = \{s_{i1}, \ldots,
	s_{in_i}\}$ for each axis $i \in \{1,\ldots,p\}$. Start generating the tree from the
root, descending as nodes are added. At each new node, consider the subset of splitting
points which are available in its assigned subregion along each axis, and keep only the
axes with at least one split available. If there are none, the node is a leaf. If there
are any, decide with probability $P_d = \alpha/(1 + d)^\beta$ if the node is nonterminal,
i.e., if it should have children, where $d$ is the depth of the node, $d=0$ at the root,
and $\alpha \in [0, 1]$ and $\beta \in [0,\infty)$ are two fixed hyperparameters. If the
node is nonterminal, its splitting axis is drawn uniformly at random from the allowed
ones, and the splitting point along the chosen axis is drawn uniformly at random from the
available ones.

\paragraph{Prior over leaf values}

If a node terminates, its leaf function value is sampled from a Normal distribution with
mean $\mu_\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma_\mu$, independently of other leaves. This
defines $p(M\mid T)$.

\paragraph{Complete regression model}

The BART regression model uses a sum of $n_\text{tree}$ independent trees, each
with the prior above, plus the usual error term:
%
\begin{align}
	%
	 &
	y_i = f(\mathbf x_i) + \varepsilon_i
	, \qquad
	f(\mathbf x) = \sum_{j=1}^{n_\text{tree}} g(\mathbf x; T_j, M_j)
	, \label{eq:bartdef} \\
	%
	%
	 &
	\varepsilon_i \mid\sigma \overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}\sim \mathcal \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2)
	, \qquad
	\frac{\lambda\nu}{\sigma^2} \sim \chi^2_\nu
	,                    \\
	%
	%
	 &
	p(\{T_j\}, \{M_j\}) = \prod_{j=1}^{n_\text{tree}} p(M_j \mid T_j) p(T_j).
	\label{eq:bartdef2}
	%
\end{align}
%
The free hyperparameters in this model are:
%
\begin{itemize}

	\item The splitting grid. The two common defaults are at most 100 equally
	      spaced splits along each $\mathbf x$ axis in the range of the data, or at
	      most 100 split midway between each observed value along each $\mathbf x$
	      axis.

	\item The number of trees $n_\text{tree}$, default 200. Although indicated with
	      ``$m$'' is the original notation, in this manuscript we use $n_\text{tree}$ for
	      clarity.

	\item $\alpha$ and $\beta$, regulating the depth distribution of the
	      trees. Default 0.95 and 2.

	\item $\mu_\mu$ and $\sigma_\mu$, which set the prior mean and variance
	      $m\mu_\mu$ and $n_\text{tree}\sigma_\mu^2$ of $f(\mathbf x)$. By default set
	      based on some measure of the location and scale of the outcome data.

	\item $\nu$ and $\lambda$, which regulate the prior on the variance of the error
	      term. By default $\nu=3$, while $\lambda$ is set to some measure of squared
	      scale of the data.

\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The BART MCMC}

Next, we give an overview of the BART MCMC, i.e., the algorithm used to ``learn
the trees''. In this explanation we assume understanding of the basic techniques
used in Bayesian inference; see \autoref{sec:bayesrecap} for a recap of the
topic. For more details see \citet{chipman1998,chipman2010}, \citet[\S
	A]{kapelner2016}, \citet[ch.~5]{daniels2023}, and \citet[\S2.1.4,
	p.~5]{tan2019}.

BART uses Metropolis and conjugate sampling within a Gibbs sampler. The sampler
scans over trees. On each tree, the structure $T_j$ is updated with Metropolis,
conditional on the other trees, but marginalizing the leaves $M_j$ of the
current tree. Then, $M_j$ is conjugately sampled conditional on the other trees
and the updated $T_j$. After scanning all trees, $\sigma^2$ is conjugately
sampled, conditional on the rest. More in detail, each iteration of the MCMC
proceeds as follows. A random small modification is proposed for $T_1$,
proposing to grow a leaf into a node with two leaves, to prune two sibling
leaves making their parent a leaf, or other more complex moves. The change is
either accepted or rejected. Then, $M_1$ is sampled given the potentially
modified $T_1$; this step is a linear regression. This is repeated for all trees
in order. Then $\sigma^2$ is sampled with the trees fixed; its posterior is
approximately centered on the current residual variance.

Based on our general experience with BART and with developing the implementation
described in this paper, the acceptance of the tree structure Metropolis step is
typically \SI{10}\%--\SI{30}\% \citep[see also][p.~891]{pratola2016}, so on
average the whole forest moves every 3 to 10 MCMC iterations. Pathological cases
can lower this acceptance to very small values. A rule of thumb on the number of
samples is to run the chain for 2000 samples and discard the first 1000
(differently from the defaults of popular BART packages, which range from 100 to
500).
%
% default burn-ins on 2024-10-30:
% 100 in BART, dbarts::bart, BayesTree
% 250 in bartMachine
% 500 in dbarts::bart2
%
See \citet[fig.~7, p.~20]{tan2024} for an analysis of the benefit of running
multiple chains. It is useful to run at least 2 chains and compare the
distributions of the function value at a few points; if the distributions do not
coincide, it may be useful to burn more initial samples. Each tree should not
end up with more than 10 leaves, the algorithm is meant to work with shallow
trees. If the trees grow deeper, increase the number of trees.

This MCMC is quite complex, and moves the tree structures slowly. In practice in
most problems it will not reach convergence, i.e., it won't sample the tree
structures from their actual posterior. However, the function values also depend
on the leaves, which are re-sampled each time from scratch instead of updated
with Metropolis, so the quality of the MCMC samples is sufficient for
prediction, even if they are not representative of the precise tree distribution
down to every detail. See \citet[p.~12]{ronen2022} and \citet[p.~4]{tan2024}.

\section{Implementation of BART for GPU}
\label{sec:impl}

This is the principal contribution of this paper. The implementation described
here corresponds to version 0.9.0 of the Python package \texttt{bartz}
\redactedcite{\citep{gattocrucco_2026_19661598}}. The software is a pure Python package based
on the machine learning library JAX \citep{bradbury2018}, which provides a
computing toolset built around Numpy-like \citep{harris2020} tensor objects.

Amongst other array-oriented ML frameworks, what makes JAX attractive for
implementing BART is its focus on XLA compilation, useful to write the somewhat
convoluted BART MCMC in simple Python code while avoiding scripting overhead,
and getting good performance both on CPU and GPU. XLA imposes the constraint
that all tensors and their sizes, and often in practice the full sequence of
operations, have to be specified statically, in advance, before running the
program. Thus, our BART implementation in JAX is branchless: the MCMC loop
always executes the exact same set of operations, irrespectively of the current
state.

Near-branchlessness is what allows XLA to produce performant programs
automatically. However, the BART MCMC is not a natural fit for branchlessness:
the tree structures change at each iteration, with nodes added and removed, and
the set and number of datapoints assigned to each node altered. To shoehorn this
algorithm into JAX, we make two major deviations from other implementations:
1)~we fix a maximum depth for the trees, and then write tree-traversing
operations as if all trees were always maximal, with arithmetic that masks aways
operations on nodes that are not actually used; 2)~we always scan through all
datapoints, even for operations that would require only the subset of datapoints
associated with a given node. These sweeping de-optimizations are feasible
because, like boosted trees, the point of BART is aggregating ``weak learners'',
so trees are shallow \citep[see, e.g.,][\S6]{chipman2010}. In our
implementation, we set a default maximum number of 6 tree levels, i.e., at most
32 leaves per tree, which is more than sufficient.

To represent the trees as fixed-size matrices, we use a heap, as illustrated in
\autoref{fig:heaptree}.
%
\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{heaptree}
	\caption{\label{fig:heaptree} Heap representation of a decision tree, with
		the maximum depth set to 3.}
\end{figure}
%
Each tree is represented by 3 arrays, separately holding the axis and splitting
point for decision nodes, and the value for leaf nodes. The arrays are fixed in
size, and each index corresponds to a potential node in the tree. The indices
are assigned to the (potential) tree nodes in order, starting from 1, top to
bottom, left to right (like reading text), imagining a maximal (full, balanced)
binary tree with the maximum allowed depth. The entries corresponding to nodes
not actually present in the tree are ignored, as are the axis and cutpoint
entries (resp.\ the leaf value entry) corresponding to leaf (resp.\ decision)
nodes. We fix the convention that the cutpoints start from 1, and that a 0 value
in the cutpoint array entry marks the node as a leaf. The set of trees is then
represented by 3 matrices where each row is a single tree array. See
\autoref{sec:impldetails} for an analysis of the memory usage of this
representation.

Next, we illustrate how to traverse a tree in a branchless fashion, in
\autoref{alg:traverse}. By ``traversing'' a decision tree we mean finding the
%
\begin{algorithm}[t]
	\KwData{Predictors $\mathbf x$, axis vector $\mathbf a$, cutpoint vector $\mathbf s$}
	\KwResult{Heap index of the leaf $\mathbf x$ falls into}
	leaf\_found = False\;
	index = 1\;
	\For{i = 1 \KwTo $D - 1$}{
		axis = $a_\text{index}$\;
		split = $s_\text{index}$\;
		leaf\_found = leaf\_found OR (split = 0)\;
		child\_index = $2 \times \text{index} + (\text{1 \emph{if} $x_\text{axis} \ge
		\text{split}$ \emph{else} 0})$\;
		index = index \emph{if} leaf\_found \emph{else} child\_index\;
	}
	\Return index\;
	\caption{\label{alg:traverse} Branchless traverse of a decision tree}
\end{algorithm}
%
leaf associated with a given predictors unit $\mathbf x$. This algorithm is used
after training to compute predictions. To fix in advance the sequence of
operations irrespectively of the actual position of the leaf in the tree, we
always loop down to the maximum depth, but we carry a flag to indicate whether
the leaf has already been reached or not, to mask away the redundant operations
on levels below the leaf. JAX automatically vectorizes the code: each operation
in the traverse is repeated for all points and all trees, with scalar variables
becoming vectors or matrices as needed.

In the following we will highlight a few other implementation choices and
important points, without giving a full description of the training algorithm
due to its complexity. For readers familiar with boosting, keep in mind that
this MCMC is kind of like boosting but with a fixed set of trees, cycling back
in a loop to progressively update the trees in small increments. Suggestively,
it could be called an ``interleaved stochastic tree boosting'' algorithm.

\paragraph{Representation of predictors}

Each predictor $x_i$ can be replaced by its index into the splitting grid. Since
the number of cutpoints rarely exceeds 255 in practice (the default in BART
packages is 100), each predictor then requires only 1 byte, for a total of $np$
bytes for the matrix of predictors. This quantization is important because the
whole matrix has to fit into GPU memory. This is the same strategy used by,
e.g., LightGBM \citep{ke_lightgbm_2017}.

\paragraph{Tree structure proposal}

We have to propose a modification to each tree structure as moves for the
Metropolis steps. The original BART picks at random amongst 4 types of moves:
GROW (add two children to a leaf node), PRUNE (remove two sibling leaves), SWAP
(permute the parameters of two directly connected decision nodes), and CHANGE
(change the parameters of a decision node) \citep[\S5.1, p.~940]{chipman1998}.
Following the implementation of the \texttt{BART} package, we use only GROW and
PRUNE for simplicity \citep[\S C, p.~57]{sparapani2021}. To stay branchless, we
have to execute all the calculations for both moves, even if we end up using
only one. GROW and PRUNE have the nice properties that the modifications can not
overlap in the tree arrays, and that the tree proposed by GROW contains the
initial tree which in turn contains the tree proposed by PRUNE. This allows to
share the memory and most of the calculations between the two moves, and
simplifies the updates to the indices cache (described below) and the running
residuals.

\paragraph{Traverse indices cache}

The indices that, for each tree, indicate in which leaf each datapoint falls
into, are used to compute the acceptance probability of the Metropolis steps,
and to sample the leaves. At each MCMC iteration, these indices change in
limited ways due to the simplicity of the GROW and PRUNE moves, so it is
convenient to keep the indices in memory rather than recompute them from scratch
each time with \autoref{alg:traverse}. If the maximum depth of the trees $D$ is
$\le 8$, the indices fit into a byte, so this cache uses $n \times
	n_\text{tree}$ bytes. Together with the $n\times p$ predictors matrix, this is
the bulk of the irreducible memory usage of this algorithm, and it is an
additional memory requirement compared to (non-Bayesian) boosting. We estimate
removing this cache would slow training down by 2--3x.

\paragraph{Parallelization along trees}

The key steps of the MCMC that are computed in parallel across trees are:
%
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Propose tree structure moves.
	\item Update the tree indices cache.
	\item Sample centered full-conditional leaves.
	\item Compute most of the terms in the Metropolis acceptance probabilities.
\end{enumerate}

Instead, the computations that have to follow the ordered tree scan are:
%
\begin{enumerate}[start=5]
	\item Sum the partial (i.e., all trees less the current one) residuals
	      falling in each leaf (an indexed reduce); this is the main bottleneck.
	\item Complete the calculation of the Metropolis acceptance probability and
	      accept/reject the move.
	\item Compute the posterior mean of the leaves to un-center the sampled leaf values.
	\item Update the running residuals.
\end{enumerate}

These steps can not be run in parallel for all trees due to the cyclical
dependence between running residuals and the acceptance status of each move.
Since this part includes two $O(n)$ operations (residuals reduction and update),
it can be parallelized effectively only if $n$ is large enough. As shown in
\autoref{sec:perf} below, this can be a problem in practice, though
we mainly care about accelerating BART on large datasets.

\paragraph{Additional features}

In addition to the vanilla BART model and MCMC, \texttt{bartz} currently
implements classification with probit link, variable selection
\citep{linero2018}, data parallelism (shard examples across devices)
\citep{pratola2014}, chain parallelism (run independent MCMC chains across
devices), multivariate outcomes, and heteroskedasticity with fixed weights.

\section{Performance measurements}
\label{sec:perf}

We measure the running time and the prediction error of our implementation
together with existing packages, on simulated data. We run the benchmarks on
synthetic data to conveniently set training set size $n$ and number of
predictors $p$ while also controlling the signal-to-noise ratio. The data
generating process (DGP) is the following sparse quadratic model:
%
\begin{align}
	X_{ij}                               & \overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}\sim U(-\sqrt 3, \sqrt 3), \quad i = 1, \ldots, n, \quad j = 1, \ldots, p,               \\
	\beta_j                              & \overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}\sim N(0, \sigma^2_\text{lin} / p),                                                      \\
	P_{jj'}                              & = \begin{cases}
		                                         1 & \min(|j-j'|,p-|j-j'|) \le q/2, \\
		                                         0 & \text{otherwise,}
	                                         \end{cases}, \quad q \bmod 2 = 0, \quad q < p,                                                                  \\
	A_{jj'}                              & \sim P_{jj'} N(0, \sigma^2_\text{quad} / (p (4/5 + q))),                                                          \\
	Y_i                                  & \sim N\left(\sum_j \beta_j X_{ij} + \sum_{jj'} A_{jj'} X_{ij} X_{ij'}, \sigma^2_\text{eps}\right), \label{eq:dgp} \\
	E[\operatorname{Var}[Y_i|X,\beta,A]] & = \sigma^2_\text{lin} + \sigma^2_\text{quad} + \sigma^2_\text{eps}, \quad \text{(expected population variance)}   \\
	\operatorname{Var}[Y_i]              & = E[\operatorname{Var}[Y_i|X,\beta,A]] + \sigma^2_\text{quad} / (4/5 + q), \quad \text{(prior variance)}          \\
	\sigma^2_\text{lin}                  & = \sigma^2_\text{quad} = \sigma^2_\text{eps} = 1/3, \quad q = 2.
\end{align}

\paragraph{Hyperparameter matrix}

The number of predictors and the number of trees influence running time and
memory usage, so we repeat the benchmark on a $2\times 2$ grid of settings:
low/high p $\times$ low/high number of trees. As ``low'' settings we use $p=100$
or $p=50$, $n_\text{tree} = 200$, while as ``high'' we make the parameters grow
proportionally to $n$ with $n/p = 10$ or $20$ and $n/n_\text{tree}=8$. The
number of trees being proportional to $n$ is an unusually high setting. These
combinations are not optimal for inference, they are simple extreme cases to
explore the computational complexity of the algorithms. The ``low-low'' setting
is the one closer to common usage.

\paragraph{Timing measurements}

We pit our implementation \texttt{bartz} on CPU and GPU against \texttt{dbarts}
(the fastest BART implementation), \texttt{XGBoost}, and \texttt{catboost}. Our
CPU is an Apple M1 Pro with 16 GB RAM, while the GPU is a Nvidia RTX Pro 5000
Blackwell with 48 GB RAM. All methods that support it are allowed to use up to 6
cores on CPU. For all software packages, we run their setup and pre-processing
step separately, before starting the timer. This includes data pre-processing,
and XLA compilation for \texttt{bartz} specifically. The configurations are
mostly left to their default values. We clock a full iteration (a sweep through
all trees), taking the best of three runs. For BART packages, we repeat the run
starting from the final state of the previous run. For gradient boosting
packages, each run starts fresh. While \texttt{bartz} is branchless,
\texttt{dbarts} is not, and given the state dependence, \texttt{dbarts} timing
is less reliable. Since we take best of three, the bias should be in the
conservative direction of making \texttt{dbarts} appear faster.

Note that BART requires 1000--2000 iterations to produce a result, while
gradient boosting one iteration, though more runs are common to cross validate
or estimate uncertainty. We do not pre-multiply the results by a correction
factor because it would reduce precision of exposition, since the number of
iterations is not fixed for any method; the reader will have to keep into
account these factors to interpret the results.

The results with the ``low-low'' settings $p=100$, $n_\text{tree}=200$ are shown
in \autoref{fig:single}, left panel (complete results in \autoref{fig:time-all}
in the appendix). On CPU, \texttt{dbarts} is 3--4 times faster than
\texttt{bartz} at low training set size $n$, then \texttt{bartz} takes over at
$n\approx \num{10000}$ and maintains a 2x advantage. On GPU, \texttt{bartz}
switches from a constant to a linear regime around $n \approx
	\numrange{100000}{1000000}$, highlighting the tree-serial bottleneck that
requires parallelization along $n$ discussed in \autoref{sec:impl}. At high $n$,
\texttt{bartz} on GPU is 100x faster than both itself on CPU and XGBoost on GPU.
(Notably, XGBoost is only 20x faster than itself on GPU vs.{} CPU.) Considering
a BART run with 2000 MCMC iterations, it would then be 20x slower than a single
XGBoost run GPU vs.{} GPU, while on CPU vs.{} CPU it would be 100x slower. (Note
again: these figures do not include predictor quantization.)

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

We provide the first GPU implementation of BART as a Python package. On large
datasets, we achieve both state of the art on CPU and a $\mathord\approx 100$x
further speedup on GPU (with some given cpu/gpu models). This makes 2000
iterations of the BART MCMC about 20x slower than one XGBoost run (excl.\
quantization, same number of trees), while the analogous factor on CPU would be
100x. Beside the absolute speedup, this shows that BART parallelizes better than
non-Bayesian tree learning. On this point, note that ours is a pure Python
package, simpler than most other BART implementations, and not particularly
optimized (no custom kernels, no people with GPU experience involved). XBART,
the previous most successful attempt at substantially accelerating BART,
achieved a 20--30x speedup, but at the cost of substantial changes and
complication, without actually accelerating the full BART MCMC.

This speedup significantly increases the scope of application of BART. While our
ambition would ultimately be to make Bayesian methods as common as non-Bayesian
methods in this class, we note that raw speed is not sufficient, as shown by the
surprisingly shy adoption of XBART: popular gradient boosting libraries are
feature-packed high-quality software packages. While presently \texttt{bartz}
would most likely lack desired additional features, robustness, and
optimizations in many workflows based on non-Bayesian methods, we hope that this
result will create interest in this methodology outside of its academic
Statistics bubble, eventually leading to increased support for better
implementations.

We wish to also highlight some limitations of BART on GPU in absolute terms
instead of relative to non-Bayesian methods. BART is quite good at dealing with
high-$p$ data and variable selection, so it is a natural question how much this
GPU implementation could push BART up along $p$ rather than $n$. Alas, our
implementation runs out of GPU memory when the number of predictors $p$ or the
number of trees are high (see \autoref{sec:gpumem} in the appendix). We were not
able to sidestep this limit. We already quantize predictors and tree indices to
1~byte. The indices cache could be dropped or batched from CPU, but the
algorithm requires random access to the predictors. For the same reason, data
parallelism is possible only sharding $n$, not $p$, thus, if multiple GPUs were
employed to keep a high-$p$ design matrix in memory, each GPU would end up with
a small $n$, blocking effective parallelization.

This indicates that, beside optimization at high $n$, low-$n$ speed-ups are also
valuable. A potential solution could be to implement XBART on GPU: since it
scans through predictors or subsets of predictors, instead of accessing them at
random, we expect it would allow to complement parallelization/sharding along
$n$ with parallelization/sharding along $p$. Another potential solution could be
to devise variations of the BART model and MCMC that do not require fully random
access to all predictors in all trees. Yet, maybe a simpler direction would be
to profile and optimize our implementation.

\section{Recap on Bayesian inference with MCMC}
\label{sec:bayesrecap}

In the Bayesian paradigm, the parameters of the model (and consequently the
predictions) are inferred from the data by conditioning the prior probability
distribution on the observed values of the data, obtaining a posterior
distribution for the parameters. The BART model definition in
Equations~\ref{eq:bartdef}--\ref{eq:bartdef2} can be expanded into a joint
probability density function of the data and parameters $p(\mathbf y, \sigma,
	\{T_j\}, \{M_j\})$; the posterior $p(\sigma, \{T_j\}, \{M_j\}\mid \mathbf y)$
is, up to a constant multiplicative factor, the same expression but with
$\mathbf y$ held fixed at the observed values. This distribution expresses the
degree of belief in each possible combination of parameter values being the
correct one; e.g., the mode or the mean of the distribution could be used as
estimates of the parameters.

\paragraph{MCMC}

Even though the posterior $p(\sigma, \{T_j\}, \{M_j\}\mid \mathbf y)$ could be
written down explicitly, it is a long and complex formula that is in practice
impossible to interpret. To represent the posterior in a legible way, the most
common technique is Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC): setting $\theta=(\sigma,
	\{T_j\}, \{M_j\})$ for brevity, it is possible to define an iterative numerical
procedure that, starting from an initial choice of parameter values
$\theta^{(0)}$, evaluating numerically the posterior density function
$\theta\mapsto p(\theta|\mathbf y)$ only in some points, picks a new set of
values $\theta^{(1)}$, and so on $\theta^{(2)}, \theta^{(3)}, \ldots$, yielding
overall a sequence of parameter values which is a random sample from the
posterior. Then, e.g., the mean of the distribution is approximated by the
average of these samples. See \citet{brooks2011} for a reference on MCMC.

The samples produced by MCMC are only asymptotically correct: the chain takes
some time to ``forget'' the arbitrary initial state $\theta^{(0)}$ and start
sampling from the target distribution $p(\theta|\mathbf y)$. They are also not
independent, as each sample is picked starting from the previous one. In
practice, some of the initial samples must be discarded, and even after that,
the remaining number of MCMC samples must be higher by some factor than what's
sought.

\paragraph{Metropolis and Gibbs sampling}

A common general way of building a MCMC is the Metropolis-Hastings (MH) class of
samplers. A MH sampler requires to define a \emph{proposal distribution}
$p(\theta';\theta)$. At MCMC step $k$, the current values of the parameters
$\theta^{(k)}$ in the chain are plugged as parameters of the proposal as
$p(\cdot;\theta^{(k)})$. A random draw $\tilde\theta^{(k+1)}\sim
	p(\cdot;\theta^{(k)})$ from the proposal gives potential new parameter values
$\tilde\theta^{(k+1)}$. Then a criterion decides if the new proposed values are
accepted (keep new values, $\theta^{(k+1)} = \tilde\theta^{(k+1)}$) or rejected
(keep current values, $\theta^{(k+1)} = \theta^{(k)}$), with a biased coin flip
that depends on the posterior and favors the choice of $\theta^{(k+1)}$ that
makes the posterior density higher. In other words, the sampler tries to move
one step at a time in parameter space, picking a move at random, testing the
ground, and deciding whether to proceed in that direction, based on how much the
probability increases going there.

A special case of Metropolis-Hastings is Gibbs sampling. Split the parameters in
two subsets $\theta = (\theta_a, \theta_b)$. If we adopt a MH proposal that
proposes changes to $\theta_a$ only, keeping $\theta_b$ fixed, from the
\emph{full conditional} distribution $p(\theta_a|\theta_b,\mathbf y)$, it turns
out that the MH acceptance probability is always~1 and the move is always
accepted. Alternating this procedure between $\theta_a$ and $\theta_b$ yields a
valid sampler for the full $\theta$. Gibbs sampling allows to nest sampling
strategies, by applying them one parameter at a time with the full conditional
as posterior distribution.

With these notions in hand, the high level description of an iteration of the BART MCMC is
%
\begin{align}
	\text{loop on tree $j$: } & \begin{cases}
		                            T_j \sim \text{MH on } p(T_j\mid\mathbf T_{-j},\mathbf M_{-j},\mathbf y,\sigma^2), \\
		                            M_j \sim p(M_j\mid\mathbf T,\mathbf M_{-j},\mathbf y,\sigma^2),
	                            \end{cases} \\
	\sigma^2                  & \sim p(\sigma^2\mid\mathbf T,\mathbf M,\mathbf y).
\end{align}


\section{Further details on the implementation}
\label{sec:impldetails}

\paragraph{Size of the tree representation}

With reference to \autoref{sec:impl}: the heap representation of the trees
wastes most of the array entries. However it turns out to use about the same
memory as other common alternatives. The maximum number of nodes is $2^D - 1$,
so the heap size is $2^D$ (index 0 is unused). Since the nodes at maximum depth
can only be leaves, the axis and cutpoint arrays are only $2^{D-1}$ long. Using
32 bit integers and floats, the total size in bytes is $4 \times (2^{D-1} +
	2^{D-1} + 2^{D}) = 2^{D+3}$ bytes. If $D=6$, that's 512 bytes. Consider instead
a representation in C/C++ that used separately allocated linked node objects.
Each node contains allocation metadata (16 bytes), axis (4), cutpoint (4), leaf
value (4), and pointers to children ($2\times 8$), for a total of 44 bytes per
node, rounded to 48 for alignment. This matches the size of the heap if there
are 10 nodes per tree on average, and it's also not contiguous in memory. Other
possible linked schemes, and those used in existing implementations, similarly
consume a lot of memory in scaffolding; for example, \texttt{BART} uses 64 bytes
per node. % see BART 2.9.6, files src/tree.h:100, src/tree.cpp:56

\paragraph{Numerical error due to caching residuals}

Since the residuals are cached and updated according to tree modifications,
instead of recomputed, a potential problem is the accumulation of numerical
error. The values of the residuals and their updates are similar across
datapoints and trees, so, with 32 bit floats, each MCMC iteration adds a
relative numerical error with standard deviation $\approx \num{1e-7} \sqrt
	{n_\text{tree}}$. This is not a problem for all current BART applications, but
it would be with 16 bit floats, which might be an option to further accelerate
the implementation.

\paragraph{Effect of limited GPU memory}

A striking feature of the speed benchmarks in \autoref{fig:time-all} is that
with high $p$ or $n_\text{tree}$ the \texttt{bartz-P5000} line terminates at
small $n$ like the other methods despite being faster. Our benchmark harness
increases $n$ until timeout or out-of-memory error, so this indicates that
\texttt{bartz} is running out of GPU memory. The memory installed on GPU cards
is fixed, it can't be expanded, and there is no swap, so this is a hard limit.
The speed premium of the GPU kicks in only at high $n$, so this memory limit
indirectly limits the speed gain. The practical consequence is that running the
algorithm in ``high'' $p$ or $n_\text{tree}$ settings allows a speed gain of at
most about 20x rather than the asymptotic 100x.

\paragraph{On increasing the number of trees}

Next, we discuss how the speed of this implementation allows to run BART with
many more trees than usual, which is an unexplored route in the BART literature,
and potentially connects to various scaling behaviors of BART. Our a priori best
guess is that, since 1) the total number of leaves in the set of trees is the
effective number of parameters of the regression model, 2) the optimal number of
parameters will in general increase with $p$ and $n$, and 3) the trees ought to
remain shallow, then: the number of trees should increase up to linearly with
$\max(n, p)$.

We look at our RMSE measurements to get some empirical evidence that increasing
the number of trees is useful. Consider the left column of panels in
\autoref{fig:rmse-all}. They both have a ``high'' $p$ setting ($n = 20p$),
while differring in the number of trees. In the bottom panel, at fixed
$n_\text{tree}=200$, the RMSE increases faster with $n$, reaching at some point
the population variance, indicating useless predictions.

Nobody, that we know of, ever explored the effect of increasing the number of
trees this much. At ``high'' $n_\text{tree}$, the series stop at $n\approx
	8000$, which means $n_\text{tree}\approx 1000$. We've never seen more than 200
trees in the literature, but for the experiments in \citet[fig.~6,
	p.~286]{chipman2010} and \citet{collins_improved_2023} who reach resp.\ $m=300$
and $m=800$. The GPU example script that we provide in the documentation of
\texttt{bartz} runs with \num{10000} trees by default.

The number of trees bears on the acceptance of the BART MCMC, together with the
level of noise in the data. If the number of trees is too small, each tree has
to grow deep to do its part in explaining the patterns in the data, and the MCMC
gets stuck because ungrowing the trees is disfavored, and so they can't be
changed in little steps; while with enough trees, each tree has to explain only
a small part of the variation, so the prior prevails and keeps them shallow. The
level of noise (i.e., unpredictable variation) has a similar effect
\citep{pratola2016}: if the data is precisely predictable (for concreteness,
picture $\operatorname{Std}[\mathbf y]/\sigma = 100$), the trees quickly grow to
explain the variation, the error variance is shrunk accordingly, and once it is
small, any small change that doesn't preserve a nigh-perfect data fit is too
disfavored, so the MCMC gets stuck. Thus the signal/noise ratio (SNR) is an
important influence on the optimal number of trees; high SNR datasets may need
more trees.

These considerations, together with the absence of high-$n_\text{tree}$
experiments, suggest this as an interesting area to explore, made accessible by
this work as it alleviates the computational burden. Consider these examples
from the literature, where it appears the authors were not considering
increasing the number of trees as an option:

\begin{itemize}

	\item \citet[\S4.1]{ronen2022} show numerically how the convergence of the
	      MCMC degrades at high $n$. Their experiment is already computationally
	      demanding, and they stick to the default 200 trees, but a higher number of
	      trees might improve the mixing.

	\item \citet{pratola2016} develops complex Metropolis proposals to increase
	      acceptance in high-$n$, low-$\sigma$ datasets; a simpler alternative
	      solution might be again to increase the number of trees.

	\item \citet{tan2024} study how the MCMC convergence deteriorates increasing
	      $n$; they also provide some evidence on the usefulness of more trees in
	      fig.~4, p.~16, but trying only up to 10 trees at $p=10$. They seemingly
	      consider various more complex alternatives more interesting for improving
	      mixing than simply trying more trees.

	\item \citet{lakshminarayanan_particle_2015} introduce an alternative BART
	      MCMC algorithm, claiming that it solves scaling problems of the standard
	      BART MCMC. However, in their empirical comparisons (\S4), to fit a
	      high-dimensional dataset, they set the BART hyperparameters to make the
	      trees grow very deep, instead of simply increasing the number of trees.

	\item \citet[\S2, p.~8]{yannotty_combining_2026} express the worry that on
	      large datasets the trees will have to grow deep. In our experience, the
	      trees become immediately shallower when their number is increased.

\end{itemize}
